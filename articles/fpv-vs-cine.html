FPV vs. Cine: How Drones Changed Cinema

From helicopters and early camera drones to FPV flying through Hollywood action scenes.

Heath Lyon · November 18, 2026

When most people watch TV, drone shots are just a part of the show and blend in with the rest of the footage. But much to the annoyance of anyone who has the misfortune of watching a movie with me, I have to point out every time I see one.

The funny thing is that once you start flying drones, you begin to recognize the different ways they’re used in movies. There’s the classic slow aerial shot revealing a city or landscape, and then there’s the kind of footage that makes you wonder how the camera operator managed to survive the shot.

While both are technically drone cinematography, the difference between traditional cinematic drones and FPV is pretty significant.

And to understand why, we have to look at how drones ended up in Hollywood in the first place.

Before Drones, There Were Helicopters

Aerial cinematography is nothing new. Filmmakers have been trying to get cameras off the ground for practically as long as movies have existed.

Before drones, if a director wanted a sweeping aerial shot, they generally needed an airplane or helicopter. These aircraft allowed cinematographers to capture incredible footage, but they also came with some pretty serious limitations.

Helicopters are expensive, loud, and require a considerable amount of coordination. They also aren’t exactly ideal for flying through a doorway or following someone down a narrow hallway.

A great example of early aerial cinematography is The Shining (1980). The opening sequence follows a little yellow Volkswagen winding through the mountains while the camera floats above the road. Those shots were filmed from a helicopter, and they still look incredible today.

Eventually, filmmakers began experimenting with remote-controlled helicopters carrying cameras. Companies like Flying-Cam developed specialized aircraft that could capture shots without putting a pilot in the air.

Flying-Cam’s SARAH system became advanced enough to receive a Scientific and Technical Award from the Academy in 2014.

The technology was impressive, but these were specialized machines operated by professional crews. They weren’t exactly something the average person could pick up at Best Buy.

The DJI Revolution

Things really began to change in the early 2010s.

In 2013, DJI released the original Phantom, a small quadcopter that could carry a GoPro. Suddenly, getting a camera into the air didn’t necessarily require a helicopter, a massive budget, or a custom-built aircraft.

The early Phantoms were nowhere near as capable as modern drones. Their cameras and stabilization systems were limited, and the footage wasn’t always particularly smooth. But the technology was advancing incredibly quickly.

DJI continued developing its Phantom lineup and eventually introduced the Inspire series, which brought more professional camera systems and the ability to have separate operators controlling the aircraft and camera.

In the United States, the FAA also began authorizing commercial drone operations for film production in 2014. The regulatory changes that followed helped drones become a normal part of professional filmmaking.

By the time DJI released the Inspire 2 in 2016, drones were becoming serious filmmaking tools.

Instead of renting a helicopter to capture a dramatic reveal of a building or landscape, filmmakers could use a drone that fit in the back of a car.

Of course, simply being able to fly a camera didn’t automatically make someone a cinematographer. It just made aerial filmmaking accessible to a lot more people.

What Makes a Cine Drone Different?

When people think of professional drone footage, they probably imagine something from a DJI Mavic or Inspire.

These are what I generally think of as traditional cinematic drones, or “cine” drones.

The entire aircraft is designed around getting smooth, stable footage.

Most use a three-axis gimbal to stabilize the camera independently from the aircraft. That means the drone can tilt, turn, or make small corrections without those movements being directly transferred to the footage.

GPS positioning, obstacle sensing, automated flight modes, and sophisticated camera controls make these drones extremely useful for controlled camera movements.

If you want a slow reveal of a mountain range or a smooth orbit around a building, a cine drone is probably the tool you’re looking for.

And for a lot of filmmaking, that’s exactly what you want.

The audience isn’t supposed to think about the drone. They’re supposed to pay attention to what’s happening on screen.

But there are some things a traditional cine drone simply isn’t designed to do.

Enter FPV

FPV stands for First Person View.

Unlike traditional cinematic drones, FPV aircraft are generally flown from the perspective of a camera mounted on the drone itself. The pilot wears goggles that display a live video feed, making it feel like they’re actually sitting inside the aircraft.

Most FPV drones are built for speed, maneuverability, and direct control.

Instead of relying on GPS positioning and automated stabilization to keep the aircraft level, experienced FPV pilots fly in acro mode, manually controlling the drone’s attitude and throttle.

That makes FPV considerably harder to learn, but it also opens up an entirely different style of filmmaking.

An FPV drone can dive down the side of a building, roll through the air, skim inches above the ground, and transition between movements that would be difficult or impossible for a traditional gimbal-stabilized drone.

The camera becomes part of the aircraft’s movement instead of being isolated from it.

This is where FPV really begins to shine.

With traditional cinematic drones, the camera often feels like it’s floating above the action. With FPV, the camera can feel like it’s actually participating in it.

When FPV Made It to Hollywood

One of the most interesting developments in modern drone cinematography has been the introduction of FPV aircraft capable of carrying professional cinema cameras.

For years, a lot of FPV footage was captured using small action cameras like GoPros. They were lightweight and durable, but filmmakers working on major productions often wanted the image quality and flexibility of the cinema cameras they were already using.

Enter the cinelifter.

These are larger FPV drones built specifically to carry professional camera equipment, sometimes including RED, Sony, or other cinema camera systems.

The result is an aircraft that combines the aggressive flight characteristics of FPV with the image quality expected on professional film sets.

One of the most notable examples is Netflix’s Red Notice (2021).

The production used FPV drones carrying a RED Komodo camera to capture dynamic action sequences, including an opening shot that moves through the environment in ways traditional camera systems would have struggled to achieve.

Then came Michael Bay’s Ambulance (2022).

If you’ve watched the film, you’ve probably noticed the camera doing some pretty ridiculous things.

There are shots diving down buildings, racing through the streets, and flying alongside moving vehicles. FPV pilots including Alex Vanover and Jordan Temkin helped capture some of the film’s most memorable camera movements.

What’s particularly interesting is that these shots weren’t simply there because drones were new or exciting. They gave the filmmakers a way to move the camera that wasn’t previously practical with traditional equipment.

Of course, not every movie needs a camera doing a front flip between buildings.

But the possibilities are pretty exciting.

So Which Is Better?

This is probably the wrong question.

Traditional cine drones and FPV drones are different tools designed for different jobs.

If I’m filming a property and want a smooth, controlled shot showing the house and surrounding landscape, a traditional cinematic drone makes perfect sense.

If I want to fly through a doorway, navigate a tight space, or follow a moving subject with aggressive camera movements, FPV becomes a much more interesting option.

Even within FPV, there’s a huge difference between flying a small ducted cinewhoop through a building and flying a large cinelifter carrying a cinema camera.

The aircraft, camera, flight characteristics, and level of risk all change depending on the job.

And one thing I’ve learned is that the most complicated shot isn’t automatically the best shot.

There’s a tendency, especially when you’re getting into FPV, to want every shot to be as fast and aggressive as possible. But sometimes a simple, well-composed camera movement communicates more than diving off a building ever could.

The trick is knowing when to use each one.

The Future of Drone Cinematography

What interests me most about FPV is how much of its development has come from people building, modifying, and experimenting with their own equipment.

A lot of the aircraft used in FPV filmmaking aren’t something you can simply purchase as a complete package. They’re assembled from frames, motors, flight controllers, ESCs, cameras, and whatever other equipment the pilot needs to get the job done.

That’s a big part of what attracted me to FPV in the first place.

And while manufacturers continue developing increasingly sophisticated ready-to-fly drones, the FPV community is still experimenting with new aircraft designs, camera mounts, and ways of moving through the world.

I don’t think FPV is going to replace traditional cinematic drones, just as drones haven’t completely replaced helicopters.

But I do think filmmakers are going to keep finding increasingly creative ways to use them.

And the next time you’re watching a movie and the camera suddenly dives off a rooftop, races through traffic, or follows someone through a narrow corridor, there’s a decent chance an FPV pilot had something to do with it.

I’ll probably be the annoying person pointing it out.
