OLDER-ADULT FALLS: FREQUENCY, DANGERS, AND DESIGN CONTEXT
==========================================================
Prepared for the Heath Lyon YOLO Fall Detection case study
Sources reviewed: August 2, 2026
Population: United States adults age 65 and older

EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
Falls are the leading cause of injury for U.S. adults age 65 and older. More
than 14 million older adults—about one in four—report falling each year. CDC
estimates about nine million fall injuries annually. Falls also account for
roughly three million emergency-department visits and about one million
hospitalizations among older adults each year.

HOW OFTEN FALLS HAPPEN
- More than 14 million, or one in four, adults age 65+ report a fall each year.
- About 37% of older adults who fall report an injury that requires medical
  treatment or restricts activity for at least one day.
- That burden represents an estimated nine million fall injuries annually.
- Falling once doubles a person's chance of falling again.

SERIOUS HEALTH CONSEQUENCES
- About three million emergency-department visits each year are related to
  older-adult falls.
- About one million fall-related hospitalizations occur among older adults
  each year.
- Falls are the most common cause of traumatic brain injury.
- Nearly 319,000 older adults are hospitalized for hip fractures each year;
  CDC separately summarizes the annual burden as about 300,000 hip-fracture
  hospitalizations due to older-adult falls.
- Hip fractures can be difficult to recover from, and some people cannot live
  independently afterward.

MORTALITY TREND
Falls are the leading cause of injury-related death for adults age 65 and
older. The CDC reports that the age-adjusted older-adult fall death rate rose
21%, from 64.7 deaths per 100,000 in 2018 to 78.4 per 100,000 in 2024.

EFFECTS BEYOND THE INITIAL INJURY
Falls can cause broken bones, head injuries, reduced mobility, hospitalization,
loss of independence, and death. People who fall may also become afraid of
falling again. That fear can reduce everyday activity; lower activity can lead
to weakness and raise future fall risk.

DESIGN CONTEXT FOR A FALL-DETECTION PROTOTYPE
A computer-vision prototype may explore whether pose and movement patterns can
help bring attention to a possible event. A responsible design should:

1. Evaluate movement across time instead of classifying one image.
2. Track detection quality and expose uncertainty.
3. Use confirmation and cooldown states to reduce repeated alerts.
4. Minimize retained imagery and support privacy-aware rendering.
5. Log events for review instead of treating every signal as certain.
6. State clearly that false alarms and missed events are possible.
7. Avoid promising diagnosis, prevention, guaranteed detection, or emergency
   response unless those claims are supported by appropriate validation and
   regulatory review.

PREVENTION CONTEXT
Detection is not prevention. CDC recommends that older adults and caregivers
work with healthcare professionals on fall-risk assessment, medication review,
strength and balance, vision, footwear, osteoporosis screening, and home
hazards. CDC's STEADI resources support clinical screening, assessment, and
intervention.
